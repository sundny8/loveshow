# Chinese Number Slang Explained: 520, 1314, 88…

Chinese number slang works by homophones: **digits are pronounced so they sound like real Mandarin phrases — 520 (wǔ èr líng) sounds like “I love you” (wǒ ài nǐ), 1314 (yī sān yī sì) sounds like “forever” (yī shēng yī shì), and 88 (bā bā) sounds like “bye-bye.”** Born in the pager era of the 1990s, these codes remain everywhere in Chinese chats, red-envelope amounts, usernames and license-plate wishlists.

Here's the full decoder, organized by theme.

## Why Numbers? The Homophone Engine

Mandarin has an unusually high density of homophones — a handful of syllables cover thousands of characters. That makes numbers a natural cipher: each digit's pronunciation approximates a word, and strings of digits become sentences. When pagers and early SMS made typing Chinese characters slow, numeric shorthand exploded, and it never left. (Linguists file this under the broader phenomenon of [homophonic puns in Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophonic_puns_in_Standard_Chinese), which also power holiday taboos and lucky-number pricing.)

## Everyday Codes You'll Actually Encounter

- **666** — flooding a game chat or livestream when someone plays brilliantly. Derived from 溜 (liù, “smooth/skilled”).
- **233** — laughter; the number of a laughing emoticon on an early Chinese forum. Extend for volume: 23333333.
- **88** — “bye-bye.” Sometimes 886 (bā bā liù, “bye-bye then”).
- **555** — the sound of sobbing (wū wū wū). Self-deprecating, rarely serious.
- **995** — “save me/help me,” often about overwork; related to the term 996 (the 9am–9pm, 6-days work schedule).

## Using Number Slang as a Non-Native Speaker

Three rules keep you safe:

1. **Match the register.** 520 to a romantic interest: perfect. 748 to anyone: you just told them to drop dead.
2. **Confirm before money.** Red-envelope numerology is real — ¥5.20 delights, ¥4.44 horrifies.
3. **When in doubt, stack the classics.** 520, 1314, 666 and 88 are universally understood; deep cuts like 04551 may need explaining even to native speakers.

The payoff is real: dropping a well-placed 520 in a cross-cultural relationship shows you did your homework — and turning it into a gift (say, a [couple portrait](/en/ai-couple-portrait) captioned “5201314”, or a [custom love song](/en/ai-love-song) titled with your code) shows you meant it.

## FAQ

### What does 1314 mean in Chinese?

1314 (yī sān yī sì) sounds like 一生一世 (yī shēng yī shì) — “one life, one world,” i.e., forever. It's usually paired with 520 as 5201314: “I love you forever.”

### What does 88 mean in Chinese chat?

88 (bā bā) sounds like “bye-bye.” It's a casual sign-off, sometimes extended to 886 (“bye-bye then”).

### What does 666 mean in China?

666 (liù liù liù) sounds like 溜 (“smooth, skilled”) and means “awesome” or “well played.” It's ubiquitous in gaming and livestream chats — and unrelated to Western connotations of the number.

### Which numbers are unlucky in Chinese culture?

4 (sì) sounds like 死 (“death”) and is avoided in gifts, amounts, floor numbers and phone numbers. Combinations like 514 (“I want to die”) and 748 (“go die”) are actively rude.

### Why did Chinese number slang develop?

Mandarin's high homophone density plus the pager/SMS era of the 1990s: numbers were faster to type than characters, so sound-alike codes became a compact chat cipher — and cultural habit kept them alive.
